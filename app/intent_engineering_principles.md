### Intent Engineering Principles

| Advice Category | Principle | Why This Works | What It Is | Limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 1: Defining Purpose** | **Define the End Goal** | An AI without a clear end goal will generate output that looks productive but may not serve your actual need. Telling the AI what you're trying to produce gives it the orientation to make good decisions about scope, depth, and format throughout the task. | Tell the AI explicitly what the final deliverable is: "I need a two-page executive summary," "I need a ranked list of the five strongest candidates," "I need one paragraph I can paste into an email to the program officer." When the AI knows the destination, it can judge what to include, what to skip, and when it's done. Without this, it guesses at scope, and the guess can miss in either direction: a wall of text when you needed three bullets, or the reverse. | Users sometimes communicate the process ("use AI to analyze this data") rather than the outcome ("I need a summary of the three key trends"). If your goal sounds like a method rather than a deliverable, reframe it. |
| **Level 1: Defining Purpose** | **Identify Accountability Requirements** | Telling the AI who the output is for and what standards apply changes how it approaches the work. An AI drafting an internal brainstorm should behave differently than one drafting a regulatory submission. Without this context, the AI has no way to calibrate its level of care, precision, or conservatism. | Communicate the stakes and audience directly: "This will be reviewed by our legal team," "This is an internal draft for discussion only," "This goes to the grant review panel." When the AI knows a regulator or board will see the output, it can prioritize precision and hedging over creativity. When it knows the work is exploratory, it can be more generative. The AI can't make these trade-offs if it doesn't know who's accountable for the result. | Accountability can be diffuse in organizations, with multiple stakeholders sharing responsibility. When the audience is unclear, tell the AI to assume the higher-stakes scenario. It's easier to loosen up a careful draft than to add rigor to a casual one. |
| **Level 1: Defining Purpose** | **Establish Success Criteria** | Telling the AI what "good enough" looks like is how you communicate priorities and trade-offs. "Make this compelling" is a very different instruction than "make this accurate above all else." Without success criteria, the AI optimizes for whatever it assumes you want. Anthropic's guidance (as of October 2026): make criteria specific and measurable, and set them before you prompt. | State your success criteria in one line, then ask the AI to restate the goal or ask clarifying questions before it starts: "Summarize this report for the budget office, covering all five budget categories, in under 300 words, accuracy over readability. Before you begin, tell me in one sentence what you think I'm asking for and ask about anything unclear." The restatement catches misunderstandings while they are still cheap to fix. Keep it to 2-3 criteria: a 2025 Carnegie Mellon study found that the more requirements a prompt stacked up, the less reliably models followed each one. | Success criteria can be hard to define for creative or exploratory tasks. In those cases, define what failure looks like instead; that's often easier and communicates priorities just as effectively. |
| **Level 2: Escape Hatches** | **Tell the AI When to Stop** | A chat assistant's default is to finish the job you gave it, and how hard it pushes through ambiguity is something you set. If you never say that stopping is an acceptable outcome, it will usually pick the most plausible interpretation and keep going, even when finishing the task the wrong way is worse than not finishing it. | Include explicit stopping conditions: "If you can't verify a claim, flag it instead of guessing," "If the NOFO doesn't address this question, say so rather than inferring an answer," "If the award letter and the budget justification disagree, stop and show me both." These give the AI permission to prioritize getting it right over getting it done. Example: "Draft a budget summary from this spreadsheet. If any numbers don't match between tabs, stop and flag the discrepancy rather than choosing one." | Stop conditions work best when they're specific and concrete. Vague instructions like "be careful" don't give the AI actionable criteria for when to halt. |
| **Level 2: Escape Hatches** | **Define Human Handoff Points** | AI should know where its authority ends and a human's begins. Without explicit handoff instructions, the AI will make judgment calls it isn't qualified to make, because its only directive is to complete the task. | Tell the AI exactly where to stop and defer: "Draft the recommendation but do not send it," "Flag potential compliance issues but do not interpret the regulation." Common handoff points: before external communications, financial commitments, anything with legal implications, and anything that affects people's evaluations or standing. The simplest way to write all of this down is an always / ask first / never list, three short lines that also carry your stop conditions and boundaries: "Always quote the clause you are flagging. Ask me first before assuming a cost is allowable if the sponsor guidance is silent. Never draft language that commits the university to a date." | Too many handoff points can make the AI's output so fragmented it's not useful. Keep each heading of the list to one or two items and focus on the junctures where an AI misjudgment would be costly or irreversible. |
| **Level 2: Escape Hatches** | **Set Boundaries** | The AI doesn't know your constraints unless you state them, and it follows a constraint better when it knows the reason behind it. Anthropic's current guidance (as of October 2026) is to say what to do instead of what not to do, and to say why: the AI generalizes from a reason far better than from a bare rule. | Write boundaries as positive instructions with a reason. Instead of "Do not use numbers from the narrative," write "Use only the figures in the attached budget tab, because the narrative has not been updated since the resubmission." Instead of "Do not imply commitment," write "Phrase this as options for the AVP to consider, because the university has not agreed to anything yet." Cover both what the output should contain and how the AI should get there. | Boundaries are hard to anticipate fully before you see the AI in action. Start with the obvious ones and refine after the first run, which often reveals a gap you didn't think of. |
| **Level 2: Escape Hatches** | **Give the AI Permission to Flag Uncertainty** | By default, AI produces confident-sounding output whether it's certain or guessing. If you don't explicitly tell the AI it's allowed (or expected) to express doubt, it will fill gaps with plausible-sounding fabrications rather than admitting what it doesn't know. Giving the model explicit permission to say "I don't know" is one of Anthropic's first recommendations for reducing hallucinations (as of October 2026). | Include instructions like: "If you're unsure about any claim, mark it as unverified," "Distinguish between what the source document says and what you're inferring," "If the data is ambiguous, present both interpretations rather than picking one." Example: "Summarize the findings from this report. Where the report is ambiguous or you're uncertain about the interpretation, note it explicitly rather than presenting your best guess as fact." | Even with explicit permission, AI models may still under-report uncertainty due to training incentives toward confident, helpful responses. Treat flagged uncertainty as a minimum bound, not a complete accounting of what the AI doesn't know. |
| **Level 2: Escape Hatches** | **Define the Fallback Plan** | AI output is sometimes unusable: wrong, late, or not what you needed. If you only decide what happens then after it happens, the deadline decides for you. | Before you start, answer two questions: who does this by hand if the AI output can't be used, and by when do they need to know? "If the panel summary draft isn't usable by Thursday noon, Maria writes it from the reviewer sheets as usual." Then tell the AI so it can help you fail early: "If you can't complete this from the materials provided, say so in your first line so I can reassign it." | A fallback nobody has time to execute is not a plan. If the honest answer is "there is no backup," the task is higher stakes than it looks: build in more review, not less. |
| **Level 3: Ethical Considerations** | **Assess Bias Risk** | AI systems can introduce or amplify unfair outcomes, especially in tasks involving people, evaluations, or resource allocation. Naming the risk upfront prevents harm. | Ask: "Could this AI output disadvantage any group or individual unfairly?" Watch for it in resume screening, internal seed-grant scoring, performance evaluations, or anything sorted by demographics. Then write the guardrail into the prompt: "Score each proposal only against the published criteria. Do not treat the applicant's name, institution, or career stage as evidence of quality." | Bias is often invisible until it causes harm, and not all biases are detectable through simple review. For high-impact decisions, pair AI outputs with diverse human reviewers rather than relying on self-assessment alone. |
| **Level 3: Ethical Considerations** | **Evaluate Appropriateness** | You've already decided to use AI for this task. The question now is which parts of it stay human. Some pieces of a task call for a human author for trust, sensitivity, or regulatory reasons even when the rest is fine to delegate. | Ask: "Which parts of this would the people affected expect a person to have written?" The final recommendation on a competing proposal, the paragraph telling a PI their project was not selected. Keep those for yourself and tell the AI: "Draft the review summary, but leave the final recommendation paragraph as [HUMAN TO WRITE]; I will write that part myself." | Where the line sits varies between units and institutions. When norms are unclear, ask the people who will receive the output what they would expect. |
| **Level 3: Ethical Considerations** | **Consider Stakeholder Impact** | Ensures you think beyond your immediate needs to the broader consequences of using AI for a given task. | List everyone affected by the output: the PI, the chair, the program officer, study participants. For each, ask: "Does this AI use respect their interests, privacy, and autonomy?" Then name them in the prompt: "The PI, the chair, and the program officer will all read this. Flag anything one of them might find surprising or that reveals more than they need to see." | Stakeholder analysis can become an endless exercise. Prioritize the most directly affected groups and the most vulnerable populations rather than trying to map every possible impact. |
| **Level 4: Meta-Intent** | **Reflect on Intent Completeness** | Catches blind spots by forcing a final review of your intent before you begin - the questions you didn't think to ask are often the most important ones. | Before sending, check: Do I know the goal? The audience? Who's accountable? What's the fallback? Where are the ethical risks? What are the boundaries? Then apply the colleague test: if a colleague with minimal context would be confused by your prompt, the AI will be too. A practical fix is to open the prompt with four short lines: "Goal: ... Audience: ... Done when: ... If stuck: ..." | Checklists can create false confidence - completing every item doesn't guarantee you've covered every risk. Use the checklist as a prompt for deeper thinking, not as a substitute for judgment. |
| **Level 4: Meta-Intent** | **Revisit Intent as Context Changes** | Intent established at the start of a project may no longer hold as requirements shift, new information emerges, or stakeholders change their minds. Periodic reassessment prevents drift. | Re-check intent at natural milestones: after the first AI draft, when scope changes, or when the deadline moves. When something shifts mid-conversation, say so in the prompt rather than hoping the AI notices: "The audience is now the dean rather than the PI, and the deadline moved to Friday. Restate what you think the goal is now before continuing." | Frequent reassessment can slow momentum and create decision fatigue. Reserve formal check-ins for meaningful context shifts rather than every minor change. |

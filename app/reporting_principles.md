### Reporting Principles

| Advice Category | Principle | Why This Works | What It Is | Limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 1: Basic Disclosure** | **Know Your Sponsor's Rule** | As of October 2026 the major federal sponsors have written rules about AI in applications, and they differ. The first documentation question for a proposal, RPPR or just-in-time task is "which sponsor?" | As of October 2026: NSF encourages disclosure of generative AI use in the Project Description. NIH will not consider an application substantially developed by AI to be the applicant's original ideas (NOT-OD-25-132, effective September 2025), may refer post-award detection to the Office of Research Integrity, and expects applicants to describe their AI use. NEH requires AI-generated text in an application to be marked (footnotes or marginal notes) and can declare an unmarked application ineligible. NASA Science Mission Directorate requires a citation for any AI tool used: name, version, date and how it was used. DOE has no general rule as of October 2026, so the NOFO and your institution's policy govern. | A snapshot. Check the current notice, the specific NOFO (it can be stricter than the agency rule) and your institution's policy before deciding what to write. Clarion cannot tell you your institution's terms. |
| **Level 1: Basic Disclosure** | **State What the AI Did** | The simplest transparency: saying AI was involved and what it did. Without this, everything else is invisible. | Record (1) that AI was used, (2) which tool, (3) what it did, and (4) what the human did with the output. Example: "Microsoft 365 Copilot (institutional license, 14 October 2026) drafted the budget justification from the approved budget spreadsheet; the research administrator checked every figure and rewrote the personnel section." | Saying what the AI did does not prove the work is good. Pair it with a record of how you checked the output. |
| **Level 1: Basic Disclosure** | **Disclose Early, Not After the Fact** | Most sponsors and publishers now require or expect disclosure, and a statement made at submission preserves evidence of a defensible process. Disclosure that surfaces only after detection looks like concealment, and NIH says post-award detection may be referred to the Office of Research Integrity. | Write the disclosure statement and the internal record at the same time as the document, as part of the submission checklist, not reconstructed later. Where the sponsor has a rule (see Know Your Sponsor's Rule), follow its placement. Where the sponsor is silent as of October 2026, still keep a short internal record; "was AI used?" can be asked years after award. | Disclosure is transparency, not a cure. NIH (NOT-OD-25-132) and the Office of Research Integrity (August 2026 guidance) say that disclosing substantially AI-developed content does not make it acceptable or original. |
| **Level 1: Basic Disclosure** | **Match Disclosure to Audience** | A sponsor reading a page-limited proposal wants one clear sentence; an auditor or integrity officer wants the full record. | Keep two layers. **Submitted statement**: one or two sentences naming tool, task and human review, placed where the venue asks. Example: "Generative AI (Gemini 2.5 Pro, enterprise license) was used to tighten prose in the facilities statement; all content was written and verified by the project team." **Internal record** (proposal file or grants system): tool and tier, prompts, material provided, changes made, who verified. **Casual** (internal email): "I used Copilot to draft this; please check the numbers." | Sponsors do not want a methodology section inside a page-limited proposal, and a one-line statement is not an audit trail. Keep the full record internal and retrievable. |
| **Level 2: Process Documentation** | **Document the Prompt** | The prompt is the most direct record of what you asked. Without it, no one can judge whether the output was reasonable. | Save the prompt, or at least a paraphrase; save the exact prompt for anything submitted or audited. For routine work a summary is fine: "I asked the model to turn the PI's progress notes into the RPPR Accomplishments section in under 500 words." | For long multi-turn sessions, record the key prompts and turning points rather than every message. |
| **Level 2: Process Documentation** | **Document the Context You Provided** | What the AI knows depends on what you gave it. Without a record of inputs, no one can tell whether the output rested on a complete picture. | Note what you gave the AI and what you deliberately left out. Example: "The model was given the sponsor's subaward terms (May 2026 version) and the subrecipient's draft budget, not the prior-year audit findings or the PI's email thread." | Burdensome for routine tasks. Focus on tasks where input quality drives output quality. |
| **Level 2: Process Documentation** | **Document Where the Data Went** | Proposal content, personnel and student data can be confidential, and sponsors treat uploading someone else's application to an AI tool as a confidentiality breach. | Record (1) the tool and tier ("institutional Microsoft 365 Copilot with enterprise data protection" versus a free consumer chatbot), (2) whether confidential material entered it (unfunded proposal text, salary or effort data, student or personnel records) and (3) if the material was someone else's, under what rule you could use it. As of October 2026, NIH (NOT-OD-23-149) forbids reviewers from uploading any application content to an AI tool and treats it as a confidentiality breach; NSF forbids reviewers from uploading proposal content to non-approved tools and treats it as potentially placing the content in the public domain. Internal review panels and limited-submission committees should follow the same rule. | Record tier and date, not just the product; vendor terms and approvals change. If confidential material went into a non-approved tool, documenting it does not undo it; report it as your institution's policy requires. |
| **Level 2: Process Documentation** | **Record What You Changed** | The gap between what the AI produced and what you sent is where human judgment lives. Recording it shows you engaged with the output rather than accepting it. | Track significant changes; no line-by-line diff needed. Example: "I corrected the fringe rate the model carried over from last year, added the cost-share paragraph the NOFO requires, and removed a sentence that invented an equipment quote." | For heavily edited output, a general statement about the nature and extent of changes is enough. |
| **Level 2: Process Documentation** | **Keep the Record, Even If Incomplete** | A partial record made at the time beats a perfect one reconstructed later, and the record itself is what protects you. | Capture as you go: tool, prompt, inputs, output, changes, who checked. If verification is unfinished, record the rest and note "verification pending." As of October 2026 the Office of Research Integrity decides misconduct cases on the preponderance of evidence, not on AI detectors, so a kept record of prompts, outputs and verification steps is the best defense a PI or research administrator can have. | A partial record can look complete. Say what is missing: "Verification: not yet performed" beats silence. |
| **Level 3: Formal Reporting** | **Use a Reporting Framework** | A fixed structure keeps a formal record consistent and complete. TaMPER (Task, Model, Prompt, Evaluation, Reporting) is one such structure for the internal record or a manuscript's methods. | For work that will be audited, published or reviewed: **Task** (the goal), **Model** (tool, tier, version), **Prompt** (what you asked), **Evaluation** (how you checked), **Reporting** (where the statement went, plus this record). Use it in the proposal file, a compliance memo's appendix or a methods section; the submitted sponsor statement stays short and points to it. | Overhead for simple tasks. Not for every email, and never paste the full framework into a page-limited proposal. |
| **Level 3: Formal Reporting** | **Put the Statement Where the Venue Expects It** | A correct disclosure in the wrong place is easy to miss and can read as non-compliance. | As of October 2026, ICMJE and publishers such as Elsevier say: AI cannot be an author, the human authors remain fully responsible, AI writing assistance goes in the acknowledgments, AI used for data, analysis or figures goes in the methods, and AI use is also stated in the cover letter. For proposals, follow Know Your Sponsor's Rule. A useful check when helping a PI with an RPPR publication list. | Journal policies vary and change; the venue's current instructions win. Nature, for example, bars AI-generated images. |
| **Level 3: Formal Reporting** | **Include Negative Results** | Rejected outputs and abandoned approaches show rigor and help colleagues avoid the same dead ends. | Note when AI output was rejected and why: "The first draft of the compliance memo cited a Uniform Guidance section that does not exist; the memo was rewritten from the current 2 CFR 200 text." | Uncomfortable in a formal record. Frame failures as evidence of oversight. |
| **Level 3: Formal Reporting** | **Version Your Methodology** | AI tools change quickly and quietly; the same prompt can give a different answer next quarter. Version and date make the record reproducible. | Record tool, version or model name, tier, date and relevant settings. Example: "Drafted with Gemini 2.5 Pro through the university's enterprise license on 14 October 2026, default settings, no web browsing." This is also what NASA SMD's tool-citation rule asks for as of October 2026. | Versions are not always visible and vendors change behavior without notice. Record what you can and note uncertainty. |
| **Level 4: Institutional Standards** | **Establish Team Documentation Norms** | Office-level standards make documentation consistent and set a shared expectation of transparency. | Agree on minimums: what must be disclosed, which tools and tiers are approved, where the record lives and who reviews it. A short checklist ("Tool and tier? Prompt saved? Confidential inputs? Output verified? Sponsor rule checked?") creates accountability. | Too rigid gets ignored; too vague gets applied inconsistently. Start simple and revise with experience. |
| **Level 4: Institutional Standards** | **Create Documentation Templates** | Templates remove the blank-page problem; staff only fill in details. | Build a one- or two-sentence disclosure statement per major sponsor, an internal record form and a review checklist. Share widely. | Templates must track sponsor notices. Assign an owner and review at least annually. |
| **Level 5: Meta-Reporting** | **Review Your Documentation Practices** | Practices that fit last year's tools and sponsor rules may not fit this year's. | Periodically ask: "Is our record capturing what matters? Have our tools or the sponsor notices changed?" Then simplify, update or expand. | Schedule it infrequently (quarterly or after a major tool or policy change) and keep it practical. |
| **Level 5: Meta-Reporting** | **Documentation as Learning** | Reviewing how your office has used AI over time reveals patterns and recurring problems. | Review the archive now and then: "Which tasks does AI help with most? Where do we always have to correct it?" This speeds learning and gives evidence for institutional AI decisions. | Requires an organized record over time. Even informal notes in a shared document beat nothing. |

Sources (as of October 2026; check the current version of each): NSF notice to the research community on AI; NSF AI in merit review policy; NIH NOT-OD-25-132 and NOT-OD-23-149; ORI guidance on generative AI (August 2026); ICMJE authorship recommendations; NEH AI policy (October 2024); Elsevier generative AI policy.

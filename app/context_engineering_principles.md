### Context Engineering Principles

| Advice Category | Principle | Why This Works | What It Is | Limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 0: Before Anything Else** | **Know What Never Goes In** | Some information should never land on the AI's desk, however useful it would be as context. Once it is in a prompt, you have shared it with the AI provider, and you may not be able to take it back. | Before pasting anything, screen for: **Personal information**: names, Social Security numbers, student IDs, medical or personnel records. **Credentials**: passwords, access keys. **Regulated data**: anything covered by FERPA, HIPAA, export control (ITAR/EAR), or your institution's data classification policy. **Confidential research**: unpublished proposals and findings, proprietary methods, trade secrets. Two specifics as of October 2026: consumer AI tools (free or personal accounts) generally do not meet FERPA's "school official" requirements, so student records do not belong in them; and placing export-controlled technical data in a cloud AI tool can itself be a deemed export, because people outside the U.S. may have access to the provider's systems. When in doubt, redact or anonymize: replace names with "Person A," swap IDs for placeholders, and remove anything you would not put in an email to an outside contact. | The line between "safe" and "sensitive" is not always obvious. Your institution's data classification policy is the authority; if you do not know it, that is your first step. Some institutions license enterprise AI tools with stronger data agreements, but "enterprise" does not mean "anything goes." Check what your agreement actually covers before relying on it. |
| **Level 1: Information Selection** | **Identify What the AI Doesn't Know** | AI models have broad but shallow knowledge. They have not read your institution's policies, your sponsor's latest guidance, your internal acronyms, or anything unpublished. The biggest source of AI errors is letting it work from memory instead of from your actual documents. If you do not provide it, the AI will guess, or make something up. | Before prompting, ask: "What does the AI need to see that it couldn't possibly already know?" Then put the real thing on the desk, not your recollection of it: paste the actual policy text, the real budget figures, the specific email thread. Many tools now let you upload files, connect to document libraries, or search your organization's knowledge base during the conversation. These features exist precisely to ground the AI in facts instead of improvisation, so use them. | Users sometimes over-correct and provide everything, burying the critical material in noise (see Respect the Attention Budget). Organizations with very large document libraries eventually need tools that pull the right documents in automatically; your IT team may call these "RAG systems" or "knowledge bases." The insight is the same either way: the more real material the AI can see, the less it has to guess. |
| **Level 1: Information Selection** | **Match Context to Task Scope** | Providing too much context dilutes the model's attention; too little leaves it guessing. The right amount depends on what you are actually asking. | For a narrow question ("What's the deadline for this grant?"), provide just the relevant section of the document. For a broad task ("Summarize this policy"), provide the full text. Match the volume and specificity of context to the scope of the request. | Judging the right scope takes experience. When in doubt, start with the material you are confident matters and add more only if the output shows the AI is missing something. |
| **Level 1: Information Selection** | **Prioritize Authoritative Sources** | AI treats everything on the desk as equally true. It does not know which document is the official policy and which is a draft from three years ago. You must curate for authority. | Include only the most current, authoritative version of each document, and label it: "This is the current 2026 policy, effective July 1" or "These are the sponsor's official guidelines." Remove superseded drafts that could confuse the model. | In organizations with many document versions, finding the authoritative source can itself be a challenge. When uncertain, note the version and date so the AI can flag the ambiguity instead of silently picking one. |
| **Level 2: Organization & Formatting** | **Structure Context for Scanning** | AI models attend more reliably to well-structured input. Clear formatting helps the model find and use the right information instead of blending everything together. | Use headings, labels, and separators to organize your context: "### Policy Text", "### Key Requirements", "### Examples". Label each document with what it is, where it came from, and its date. Use bullet points for lists and tables for structured data. | Over-formatting simple inputs adds unnecessary complexity. Match the level of structure to the complexity of the context. |
| **Level 2: Organization & Formatting** | **Separate Instructions from Data** | When instructions and data are mixed together, the model may confuse what to do with what to analyze. Clear separation prevents misinterpretation. | Use explicit labels like "INSTRUCTIONS:" and "DOCUMENT TO ANALYZE:" or formatting markers like --- to separate your request from the reference material. This helps the model understand its role versus the content it should work with. | For very short prompts with minimal context, formal separation can feel heavy-handed. Use your judgment; the principle matters most when context is long or complex. |
| **Level 2: Organization & Formatting** | **Use Examples as Context** | Concrete examples teach the model what you want more effectively than abstract instructions alone. They serve as implicit formatting and quality guides. | Include 1-3 examples of the desired output alongside your instructions. For instance, if you want meeting summaries in a specific format, include a sample summary. The model will pattern-match against your examples. | Examples can anchor the model too strongly. It may copy the example's style even when the content calls for something different. Vary your examples to avoid over-fitting. |
| **Level 2: Organization & Formatting** | **Give the AI a Template to Fill In** | When you show the AI the exact structure you want (with blanks where the content goes), it produces output that matches your format on the first try instead of guessing. | Create a skeleton of your desired output with placeholders and include it in your prompt. For example, if you need a project summary, provide: "Project Title: ___, PI: ___, Budget: $___, Objectives: 1. ___ 2. ___ 3. ___". The AI fills in the blanks using the context you've provided, giving you consistent, predictable formatting every time. | Templates work best for structured, repeatable outputs. For creative or exploratory tasks, a rigid template may constrain the AI's most useful contributions. Start with a loose template and tighten it as you learn what format works best. |
| **Level 3: Window Management** | **Respect the Attention Budget** | The desk is big, but the AI's attention is not. Current flagship models can hold about a million tokens as of October 2026, roughly eight novels, and hitting that limit is not the real danger: the tool returns an error or, in chat products, summarizes or rolls off the oldest turns. The real problem is that answer quality falls long before the limit, as the desk fills with loosely related material. Researchers call this "context rot" (Chroma, 2025). | Treat attention as the budget, not space. Before loading documents, ask: "If the AI could read only ten pages closely, which ten would matter most?" Include those in full and leave the rest off the desk. Ask for one kind of thing at a time: retrieving many facts at once degrades faster than retrieving one, so split "find twenty things in this 300-page document" into several passes, each hunting for a few related items. | Context windows keep growing, and some tasks (summarizing a whole proposal, comparing two full policies) genuinely need the long document. Use the room when the task calls for it. But more material always means more competition for attention, so curation matters even when everything would fit. |
| **Level 3: Window Management** | **Put Things Where the AI Will Find Them** | Position on the desk matters. Vendor guidance converges on the same layout: long documents first, the question or instructions last. Anthropic reports that moving the question to the end can improve response quality by up to 30 percent on long-document tasks (as of October 2026). Material in the middle of a very long prompt may also get less attention than material near the start or end, the "lost in the middle" effect (Liu et al., 2023). | Arrange the desk in this order: (1) your reusable background, if any; (2) the long documents, each labeled with its source and date ("### Sponsor guidelines, Chapter II, effective May 2026"); (3) your question or instructions last. Then ask the AI to quote the passages it relied on before answering: "First quote the relevant sections, then answer." Quoting forces it to actually locate the text instead of answering from a general impression. If you provide several documents, put the most critical one first or last, not third of five. | The "lost in the middle" effect varies by model and has improved in newer ones; treat it as a reason to check placement, not a law. For short prompts, ordering matters little. The habit that pays off at every length is labeling each document and putting the question last. When the AI misses something you know you included, check where it sat on the desk before concluding the model cannot handle the task. |
| **Level 3: Window Management** | **Summarize to Cut the Noise** | Summaries are less about fitting and more about focus. A fifty-page report may contain three pages that matter to your task; the other forty-seven dilute the AI's attention. Clearing them off the desk improves answers even when the full report would fit. | Instead of pasting the whole report, write a short summary of its findings plus the specific excerpts you need word for word. Or ask the AI to summarize the document first, then start a fresh request using that summary plus the exact passages that matter. The same move rescues a long conversation that has started to wander: ask the AI to summarize where things stand, then start a new chat with that summary. (Managing conversation flow is Dialogos's lane; just know the move exists.) | Summaries lose detail. If precision matters (exact dollar figures, specific regulatory language, deadlines), include the relevant passages verbatim rather than a paraphrase. Summarize the surroundings, not the thing you are asking about. |
| **Level 4: Institutional Knowledge** | **Build Reusable Context Blocks** | Many tasks use the same background: your institution's policies, your report templates, your acronym list. Preparing these once saves time, improves consistency, and gives the AI a stable foundation it can rely on request after request. | Create a small library of context blocks: a paragraph on your institution's cost-share rules, a glossary of internal acronyms, a sample of how you format a summary. Then arrange each request as "stable block at the top, today's task at the bottom." Most AI products now have features built for exactly this: projects or workspaces (a standing set of instructions and files that every chat in that space can see), file uploads, and connectors to your document library or shared drive. Put your stable blocks there once instead of pasting them every time. | Context blocks go stale. Policies change, terminology evolves, sponsors issue new guidance. Review and update your blocks periodically, especially after major policy revisions, and date each one so the AI (and you) know how current it is. |
| **Level 5: Meta-Context** | **Audit Your Context Strategy** | What you include shapes what the AI produces. Periodically reviewing your context choices reveals blind spots, outdated information, or missed opportunities. | After getting AI output, ask: "Did the AI have everything it needed? Did it use information I wish I hadn't included? Would different context have produced a better result?" Use this reflection to improve your next attempt. | Context auditing requires comparing output quality across different context configurations, which can be time-consuming. Focus on high-impact tasks where context quality matters most. |
| **Level 5: Meta-Context** | **Evolve Context as the Task Evolves** | Tasks rarely stay static. Requirements change, new information emerges, and what the AI needs to know shifts over time. Static context leads to stale results. | At natural milestones (after initial output, when requirements change, when new data arrives), revisit your context. Ask: "Is everything here still relevant? Is anything missing that wasn't available before?" Update your context blocks and re-run if needed. | Constant context revision can slow down fast-moving work. Balance thoroughness with pragmatism. Not every update requires a full context audit. |

### Evaluation Principles

| Advice Category | Principle | Why This Works | What It Is | Limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 1: Foundational Checks** | **Check Facts, Not Just Fluency** | AI writes fluent, confident prose whether or not the content is true, because it predicts likely words rather than looking anything up. A made-up fact (a "hallucination") reads exactly like a real one. | Read AI output like an auditor, not a proofreader. Check every specific claim against an authoritative source: a sponsor deadline against the funding announcement, an F&A rate against your rate agreement, a 2 CFR 200 citation against the regulation itself, a dollar figure against your spreadsheet. If you cannot verify a claim, treat it as unverified, not as true. Tone tells you nothing: a confident answer and a hedged one need the same check. Current models decline to answer more often than older ones did ([OpenAI, 2025](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)), but hedging is unreliable in both directions: a sure-sounding answer can be wrong and an uncertain-sounding one can be right. | Checking everything is impractical for long outputs. Prioritize numbers, citations, regulatory references and anything leaving your team. Fabrications are usually mixed with real details (a real regulation with the wrong threshold), so you need subject knowledge to spot the seams. |
| **Level 1: Foundational Checks** | **Know the Types of AI Errors** | When you can name the kind of mistake, you know where to look. Catching an invented citation is a different job from catching a dropped requirement. | AI makes a few distinct kinds of mistakes. **Fabrication**: inventing a fact, source or number. **Wrong flag**: calling something a problem when it is fine (marking a salary line as over the NIH cap when it is not). **Missed flag**: failing to catch a real problem (not noticing a cost-share requirement in the announcement). **Omission**: silently skipping part of your request. **Source drift**: restating your document so the meaning changes. **Sycophancy**: agreeing with your framing even when it is wrong. After reviewing an output, walk the list and ask, "Could this one have happened here?" | A checklist tells you where to look, not what you will find. You still need to know the subject well enough to recognize a plausible fabrication or notice a missing requirement. |
| **Level 1: Foundational Checks** | **Check What's Missing, Not Just What's There** | AI fails silently. It will not tell you it skipped a requirement, dropped a key point or ignored part of your instructions. If you only check what it produced, you miss what it did not. | After reviewing the output, go back to your request and tick off each item: "I asked for five sections; are there five?" "I asked it to cover budget, timeline and personnel; are all three there?" AI often produces complete-looking output that quietly drops the hardest part. The more complex the request, the more likely something was omitted. | You can only spot omissions if you know what should be there, which means you need a specific request to begin with. If your prompt was vague, you will not know what is missing. |
| **Level 1: Foundational Checks** | **Don't Confuse Quantity with Quality** | AI produces large volumes of text quickly, which looks like thoroughness. More output is not better output; it may be more errors spread across more pages. | Before evaluating, ask: "Did I ask for this much?" If the output is longer or more detailed than needed, the excess may be filler, repetition or invented detail added to fill space. Trim to what you need and evaluate that. | Sometimes length is appropriate; a thorough analysis should be thorough. The question is whether the length serves the task or just looks impressive. |
| **Level 2: Systematic Verification** | **Test with Known Answers** | The most reliable way to gauge AI accuracy is to ask a question you already know the answer to. If it misses that, you cannot trust it where you do not know the answer. | Before relying on AI for a task, try a similar one with a known result. If it pulls your institution's on-campus F&A rate correctly from the rate agreement you provided, you can have more confidence in its other extractions from that document. If it gets the known answer wrong, lower your trust accordingly. | Known-answer tests only validate similar questions. AI can pass your test and fail the real task if it is harder or less common. |
| **Level 2: Systematic Verification** | **Check the Edges, Not Just the Center** | AI does best on common, well-documented situations and worst on unusual ones and boundary cases. Testing only the typical case gives a false sense of reliability. | Ask: "What is the strangest case this output has to handle?" and test that specifically. If AI categorized expenses, give it one that could go either way (participant support versus travel). If it summarized a policy, check whether it handled the exceptions and caveats correctly. | Finding meaningful edge cases takes domain expertise: you must know the subject well enough to predict where AI will fail, which is often the expertise the user hoped AI could replace. |
| **Level 2: Systematic Verification** | **Verify Sources Actually Exist** | AI fabricates citations with perfect formatting: real-looking authors, plausible journals, DOIs that lead nowhere. Web search and "deep research" tools do not fix this. As of October 2026, independent audits still find search-grounded models fabricate roughly 3 to 13 percent of the links they cite, and deep-research tools fabricate more per citation while producing far more citations. The [EBU's 2025 study](https://www.ebu.ch/research/open/report/news-integrity-in-ai-assistants) of AI assistants found sourcing problems were the most common failure. | Having links is not verification. For every citation, URL or DOI: open it, confirm it resolves, and confirm it says what the AI claims. Search the exact title in Google Scholar or PubMed. Check that the authors wrote it and that it appeared where stated. Common patterns: a real author on an invented paper, a real paper with its findings misstated, a real regulation at the wrong section number. A 40-item reference list is not more trustworthy than a 5-item one; citation count is unrelated to reliability. | Most critical for anything published, submitted to a sponsor, or used to justify a decision. For informal brainstorming, full verification may be more than needed. The work grows with the citation count, which deep-research tools inflate. |
| **Level 2: Systematic Verification** | **Compare Output Against Your Inputs** | When you give AI source documents (policies, spreadsheets, award notices), you expect it to use them faithfully. It can paraphrase inaccurately, add claims not in your sources, or change meaning while sounding right. | Open your source beside the output and check each substantive claim against the original. Did it carry your numbers over correctly? Did it summarize the policy accurately? Did it add anything not in your material? Budget math needs one more distinction. A number the tool produced by visibly running something (a calculation it shows, a script it ran, a spreadsheet formula it wrote and executed) is deterministic and checkable. A number it produced from memory is generated text like any other: right-sounding, which is not the same as right, and fabrication applies. So ask it to run the calculation and show the code or formula. If it did not visibly execute anything, treat the total, the fringe, the F&A or the cost-share figure as unverified and check it against your source figures or a calculation you run yourself. | Time-consuming for long outputs but essential when accuracy matters. For high stakes, check everything. For lower stakes, spot-check key claims plus every number. |
| **Level 3: Reliability & Consistency** | **Test for Consistency** | A single answer can look perfect. Run the prompt again and you may get a different one. But agreement between runs of the same model is weak evidence, because a model shares its blind spots with itself. A [2026 study](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03173v1) found that on a hard benchmark nearly half of the answers a model gave with high agreement were still wrong. Disagreement between different models is the useful signal. | Ask the same question of two or three different AI tools (different companies, not just different versions) with the same documents. Where they agree, you have modest reassurance; where they disagree, you have found exactly what to check. Focus on substance (conclusions, numbers, recommendations), not wording. Re-running the same model still has a place for high-stakes numbers, but treat agreement as "no obvious instability," not "confirmed." | Multiple tools cost time; reserve this for outputs where accuracy matters. Agreement across models is still not proof: they train on overlapping data and can share mistakes. |
| **Level 3: Reliability & Consistency** | **Use a Different AI to Check, Not the Same One** | Asking the AI that wrote something to grade it is like asking an author to proofread their own draft. Models rate their own output higher than others' (self-preference), favor whichever answer appears first (position bias) and favor longer answers (verbosity bias). These are documented, measurable biases ([Anthropic, Demystifying evals](https://anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). | For an AI second opinion, use a model from a different company than the one that produced the output. Give it a written rubric and have it grade one dimension at a time ("Are all dollar figures identical to the attached spreadsheet? Answer yes, no or unknown, and list any that differ") rather than "Is this good?" Always allow "unknown" so it is not forced to guess. Calibrate it first: hand it 5 to 10 outputs you have already graded yourself and see whether it agrees with you. If not, its grades on the rest mean little. | An AI checker inherits every weakness in this table. It can invent problems or miss real ones. It narrows where you look; it does not replace your own verification on anything that matters. |
| **Level 3: Reliability & Consistency** | **Assess Reasoning, Not Just Conclusions** | A correct conclusion reached through flawed reasoning is luck. It will not hold up when conditions change. Checking the reasoning catches errors that checking only the conclusion misses. | When AI gives an analysis or recommendation, trace the logic: does each step follow? Are the assumptions stated and reasonable? Would different assumptions change the answer? If it says a cost is allowable, check that it applied the right cost principle, not just that the answer matches your expectation. | Takes subject expertise and more time than checking conclusions. For routine, low-stakes tasks, checking the conclusion alone may be enough. |
| **Level 3: Reliability & Consistency** | **Test for Prompt Brittleness** | If a small rewording of your prompt changes the answer, the output depends on your phrasing more than on the facts. Reliable answers survive small changes in how you ask. | Rephrase: change the word order, use synonyms, ask from a different angle. If "Is this cost allowable?" and "Is there any reason this cost would be unallowable?" produce different verdicts, the output is brittle. This matters most for classifications and policy interpretations, where the right answer should not depend on wording. | Some variation is normal; AI may emphasize or organize differently. A flipped conclusion is brittleness; the same conclusion phrased differently is fine. |
| **Level 3: Reliability & Consistency** | **Watch for Sycophancy** | AI is trained to be helpful and agreeable, which means it sometimes tells you what you want to hear. It may accept your framing even when it is wrong. | Be suspicious when AI agrees with everything, especially if your prompt contained an opinion or assumption. Test it: deliberately include a wrong assumption ("since equipment over $5,000 is always unallowable...") and see whether it corrects you or goes along. If it agrees with your errors, its other agreements are worth less. | Agreement feels good, so it is hard to notice. A colleague reviewing the exchange can catch the pattern. |
| **Level 4: Workflow Integration** | **Check Output for Sensitive Data Leakage** | AI can surface sensitive information in unexpected ways: echoing personal details from your context, inferring private facts, or carrying confidential content into a summary you planned to share. | Before sharing or publishing, scan for: **names and identifiers** that should have been anonymized, **confidential details** from source documents (salaries, unfunded proposal content, reviewer comments), **inferred information** the AI filled in on its own, and **internal references** that reveal more about your organization than intended. What is routine internally may be a privacy violation externally. | Non-negotiable for anything seen outside your immediate team. For internal, low-sensitivity outputs a quick scan may do. Build the habit now so it is automatic when the stakes are high. |
| **Level 4: Workflow Integration** | **Build Evaluation into the Process** | Evaluation done as an afterthought gets skipped or rushed. Planning it from the start ensures it happens. | Before using AI for a task, decide how you will check the output and write it down: "I will open every citation," "I will verify every total against the spreadsheet," "A colleague will review the draft." Make evaluation a step in the process, not an optional add-on. | Planning means guessing failure modes before you see the output. Start basic and refine once you see which errors actually occur. |
| **Level 4: Workflow Integration** | **Match Evaluation Rigor to Stakes** | Not every output needs the same scrutiny. Over-checking low-stakes work wastes time; under-checking high-stakes work creates risk. | Classify the output. **High stakes** (going to a sponsor, an auditor or an external audience, or driving a decision): thorough checking with more than one method. **Medium stakes** (internal, informing but not deciding): spot-check key claims and all numbers. **Low stakes** (brainstorming, exploration): a reasonableness check. As of October 2026, NIH names fabricated citations as a research-integrity risk and will not treat applications substantially developed by AI as the applicant's original work ([NOT-OD-25-132](https://www.grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-25-132.html)), so anything bound for a proposal is high stakes. Check the current notice and your institution's policy. | The lines are fuzzy. When in doubt, check more. The cost of checking is almost always less than the cost of sending an error to a sponsor. |
| **Level 4: Workflow Integration** | **Pick a Scoring Scale** | "It looked okay" is not a measurement. Deciding in advance what you are scoring and how turns a vague impression into something you can compare across outputs, tools and time ([Anthropic, Develop tests](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)). | First record the model and the exact prompt so you can reproduce the test (Clarion can help). Then choose the dimensions that matter (accuracy, completeness, tone) and score each one separately. For clear-cut questions (right date? right category?) use **pass/fail**. For judgment calls (how good is this summary?) use a short labeled scale: a **5-point** scale (1 = wrong, 3 = mixed, 5 = fully accurate) leaves room for ambiguous cases; a **4-point** scale (1 = unacceptable, 4 = ready to use) forces a decision. Use labels, not bare numbers. **Clarity test:** write the criteria so two colleagues scoring the same output independently would give the same score; if they would not, the rubric is not finished. Start with 3 to 5 cases before scaling up. | This rigor is worth it for tasks you will repeat, workflows you are building trust in, or high-stakes work. For one-off, low-stakes tasks, lighter checks are fine. |
| **Level 4: Workflow Integration** | **Classify Your Errors** | Knowing that AI was wrong is less useful than knowing how it was wrong. The pattern determines whether you can work around its weaknesses or need a different approach. | For each error you find, record its type (see Know the Types of AI Errors). Pay special attention to the two directions a yes/no task can fail: **wrong flags** (it called a compliant cost unallowable) and **missed flags** (it passed a cost that should have been questioned). Over-flagging costs you review time; under-flagging costs you audit findings. The mix tells you where to put human attention. Note the pattern in a sentence: "Good at pulling dates from the announcement, unreliable on budget arithmetic." | Error patterns from a handful of cases can mislead. Revisit the classification as you accumulate examples, and expect the pattern to shift when the tool is updated. |
| **Level 5: Meta-Evaluation** | **Track Your Trust Calibration** | Trust in AI should be earned through experience, not assumed. Tracking where it succeeds and fails on your tasks calibrates your trust over time. | Keep a simple log: task type, how accurate, what you had to fix. Patterns emerge: maybe it is great at formatting and bad at numbers, or strong on summaries and weak on original analysis. Use that to decide where AI helps and where it hurts. | A log takes discipline. Even a rough mental model ("it is usually wrong about deadlines in our context") beats no calibration. |
| **Level 5: Meta-Evaluation** | **Question Your Own Evaluation** | Your evaluation is itself biased: confirmation bias (seeing what you expect), automation bias (trusting the machine) and anchoring on the AI's confident framing. A 2025 survey of knowledge workers ([Lee et al., CHI 2025](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)) found that the more people trusted generative AI, the less critical thinking they reported, and that verification drops most on tasks seen as low stakes and under time pressure, which describes much pre-award work. | Periodically ask: "Am I checking this, or confirming it?" Form your own judgment before reading the AI's explanation. Treat a looming deadline as a reason to check more, not less. Have someone who has not seen the AI output review it fresh; they catch what your primed eye misses. | Awareness of bias is not enough. External review by someone who has not seen the output is the most reliable check against your own blind spots. |

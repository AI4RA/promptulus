### Task Assessment Principles

| Advice Category | Principle | Why This Works | What It Is | Limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 1: Audit & Attestation** | **AI Has No Integrity** | The authority of professional work comes from the integrity of the person behind it. A research administrator's recommendation carries weight because they stake their professional reputation on it. A compliance officer's sign-off means something because they're accountable. AI has none of this. | Ask: "Does this task require someone to stand behind the result with their professional judgment?" AI will confidently produce wrong answers without consequence. It has no reputation, no ethics, and no skin in the game. It can inform and accelerate your work, but the final call on any decision must belong to someone with the integrity to own it. Even for Low Auditability tasks, someone should be able to say: "I reviewed this and it's correct." | This doesn't mean AI is useless, far from it. But it means AI is a tool in your hands, not a colleague with professional obligations. The distinction matters most when something goes wrong: a human can be held accountable and learn from it. AI just generates the next response. |
| **Level 1: Audit & Attestation** | **Who Needs to See the Work?** | If someone (a sponsor, auditor, or compliance officer) needs to trace how the work was done, you need methods whose steps can be shown. AI output does not document itself: unless you keep the prompts, outputs, model and version, and verification notes, there is nothing to show later. | Ask: "Could someone ask me to show exactly how I arrived at this result?" If yes, use methods where each step is visible. A spreadsheet formula can be inspected line by line; an AI answer can be inspected only if you saved what went in and what came out. For numbers, have AI write a checkable formula or script rather than produce the figure: code it runs is deterministic and checkable, while a number from its memory is generated text, and right-sounding is not the same as right. Examples: effort certification, cost share calculations, institutional assurances. | Not all "show your work" tasks are equally strict. Internal reviews may accept less documentation than federal audits. |
| **Level 1: Audit & Attestation** | **Is This Auditable?** | Tasks that could be audited need methods that give the same result every time and leave a record. A language model can give a different answer to the same question on different runs, and it keeps no record on its own. | Ask: "If an auditor asked how this was done, could I show them?" Spreadsheets, databases, and documented procedures pass this test. AI output passes only if you kept the prompt, output, model and version, and verification notes. For anything numeric (an F&A split, a cost share match), have AI write the formula or script, check it, run it, and keep that; do not accept a bare number. | Some audits focus on outcomes rather than methods. A final report may be audited for accuracy without anyone asking how it was drafted. |
| **Level 1: Audit & Attestation** | **Does Someone Attest to the Process?** | When a specific person must certify that the work was done correctly (not just that the result looks right), the process itself is the deliverable. | Ask: "Does anyone need to personally vouch for how this was completed?" If a PI certifies effort, a compliance officer signs off on a protocol, or a financial officer approves a reconciliation, those people are attesting to the process. They need to understand every step. | Attestation requirements vary by institution and sponsor. What requires sign-off at one university may be delegated at another. |
| **Level 1: Audit & Attestation** | **Know the Sponsor's AI Rules** | Sponsors now say in writing what AI may and may not touch, so a task that looked like "AI handles it" may carry originality, disclosure, or confidentiality requirements. Proposal narrative drafting is the clearest case: it is no longer a low-auditability task you can hand to AI and forget. | As of October 2026, the major sponsors have written rules. NIH will not treat applications substantially developed by AI as original work (NOT-OD-25-132, effective September 2025) and expects applicants to describe their AI use. NIH and NSF both forbid reviewers from uploading proposal content to AI tools; NSF treats that as a public disclosure. NSF now counts AI-assisted fabrication as research misconduct. NEH requires AI-generated text to be marked, and NASA requires a citation for the tool. The Office of Research Integrity says disclosure is transparency, not protection. That reviewer rule covers anyone handling someone else's proposal, including internal review panels and limited-submission committees. Ask: "Which sponsor is this for, and what does its current notice say about AI?" Then keep a record of what AI did so you can disclose it accurately. | Rules differ by sponsor and change often; check the current notice for your sponsor and your institution's own policy before you decide. Sources: NIH NOT-OD-25-132 (https://www.grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-25-132.html), NIH NOT-OD-23-149 (https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-23-149.html), NSF AI in merit review (https://www.nsf.gov/policies/ai/merit-review). |
| **Level 2: Explainability** | **Can You Show Your Work?** | If you need to explain your method to a colleague, supervisor, or sponsor, you need a method you actually understand. You can explain a spreadsheet formula. You cannot meaningfully explain what an AI model did internally, but you can show what you asked, what it returned, and how you checked it. | Ask: "If someone asked 'how did you get this number?' could I give a clear answer?" If the answer is "I asked AI and it told me," consider whether that's acceptable for this task. For internal brainstorming, maybe. For a sponsor deliverable, probably not. | Explainability is audience-dependent. "I used AI to draft it and then verified the facts" may satisfy some stakeholders but not others. |
| **Level 2: Explainability** | **Does the Method Need to Be Reproducible?** | If running the same task again must produce the same result, a language model alone is a poor fit: it can give different wording, or even different numbers, on different runs. Variability is not the same as wrongness (each run may be right and still differ), but an auditor comparing two runs sees two answers. Formulas, scripts, and documented procedures give the same answer every time. | Ask: "If I ran this again tomorrow with the same inputs, would I need the same answer?" Budget calculations, F&A splits, and reconciliations need reproducibility. First drafts and exploratory summaries tolerate variation. Middle path: have AI write the formula or script, check it once, then run it. The script is reproducible even though the model that wrote it is not. | Some tasks need approximate consistency rather than exact reproducibility. A budget narrative that's "roughly the same" each time may be fine. |
| **Level 2: Explainability** | **Would a Reviewer Accept "AI Did It"?** | This is a practical litmus test. If saying "I used AI for this" would make a reviewer pause, that's a signal the task needs more auditability. | Imagine submitting the work product and being asked about your methodology. Would "I used AI" be a satisfying answer? For formatting a bibliography: probably fine. For calculating cost sharing: probably not. Let the expected reaction guide your approach. | Reviewer expectations are evolving, and as of October 2026 several sponsors have written rules (see Know the Sponsor's AI Rules). Match current expectations rather than assuming future acceptance. |
| **Level 2: Explainability** | **Check What You're Sharing** | Every prompt you send to an AI tool is data leaving your control. Before you paste anything, ask whether the content is safe to share with a third-party service. This isn't hypothetical: people have pasted student records, personnel files, patient information, unpublished proposals, and confidential legal documents into AI tools without realizing those inputs may be logged, reviewed by vendor staff, or used to train future models. | Before using AI for a task, ask: "Does this involve personal information (names, IDs, health records, student or personnel data)?" "Is this confidential, proprietary, export-controlled, or legally privileged?" "Is this someone else's unpublished proposal?" "Would I be comfortable if this text appeared in someone else's AI output?" If the answer to any of these is yes or maybe, either anonymize the data first, use an institutionally approved tool with appropriate data protections, or don't use AI for this step. Regulations like FERPA, HIPAA, and export control rules may make this decision for you. A Low Auditability verdict never answers these questions: it says nobody needs to trace your method, not that the content is safe to paste. | It's easy to over-restrict and lose the benefits of AI entirely. Not all data is sensitive, and enterprise agreements, on-premises deployments, and tools with training-data exclusions carry different risk than free consumer tools. The goal is informed judgment, not blanket prohibition. |
| **Level 3: Process vs Output** | **Does It Matter How?** | This is the central question. If anyone cares about the method (not just the result), the task needs an auditable approach. If only the final product matters, AI can take the lead. | Ask: "Does anyone care how this work got done, or only that the final product is correct?" If the process matters, the task is process critical: High Auditability. If only the output matters, it is output-driven: Low Auditability. Within either verdict, "human-in-the-loop" is a way of working (the Human leads, AI supports pattern), not a third verdict. | Many tasks feel output-driven but have hidden process requirements. A facilities description "just needs to be accurate," but if it's in a federal proposal, someone may ask how you verified those facts. |
| **Level 3: Process vs Output** | **Is the Journey or the Destination What Counts?** | Another way to frame the same question. For some tasks, the work itself is the point (a PI reviewing effort reports). For others, only the end product matters (a reformatted reference list). | Think about what would happen if someone magically produced the perfect output without doing any of the steps. If that's fine, the task is output-driven. If skipping the steps is a problem (because the steps involve judgment, verification, or professional responsibility), the task needs more oversight. | The boundary shifts depending on context. Drafting a letter might be output-driven internally but process-relevant when it represents institutional policy. |
| **Level 3: Process vs Output** | **Is Human Thinking the Point?** | Some tasks are valuable precisely because a human applied judgment, expertise, or professional insight. Delegating these to AI doesn't just risk errors. It removes the thing that made the work valuable in the first place. | Ask: "Is someone's professional judgment, creativity, or human perspective what makes this work meaningful?" A PI reviewing effort reports is applying professional knowledge. A department administrator deciding whether a questionable charge is allowable is applying judgment. If the value comes from a human thinking about it, AI can support the work but shouldn't replace the thinking. | This principle is easy to misapply. A grants officer formatting a reference list doesn't need professional judgment for that step. Focus on where human cognition is genuinely irreplaceable, not just where a human happens to be doing it now. |
| **Level 3: Process vs Output** | **Separate What Needs a Trail from What Doesn't** | Even in process-critical workflows, some steps are auditable-by-necessity and others aren't. Identifying which is which lets you use AI where it's safe. | Map your workflow. Which steps need documentation and attestation? Those are auditable-by-necessity. Which steps are supporting work (formatting, summarizing, organizing)? Those may be fine for AI. Example: the cost share calculation needs a record; turning your finished calculation into a tidy table for the budget justification may not. But check the sponsor's AI rules before AI drafts any proposal text. | The boundaries between "needs a trail" and "doesn't" aren't always clean. A supporting step can become critical if it contains substantive claims. |
| **Level 3: Process vs Output** | **Match the Level of AI Involvement to the Task** | Different tasks call for different levels of AI involvement. Choosing the right pattern (from "AI assists" to "AI leads") depends on how much human judgment the task requires and how much oversight the stakes demand. | Consider four patterns: **Human leads, AI supports**: you do the core work; AI handles formatting, summarizing, or drafting boilerplate (the usual pattern for High Auditability tasks). **AI drafts, human verifies thoroughly**: AI produces a first version; you check every claim against source materials (for Low Auditability tasks where errors would still matter). **AI drafts, human spot-checks**: AI does the work; you do a quick reasonableness review (for low-stakes, routine tasks). **Deterministic automation**: a script, formula, or rule-based system handles the task with no AI involved at run time (for tasks needing reproducibility and auditability; AI may help write the script). Even the lightest AI pattern needs some human oversight: AI can give different answers each time and fail silently. A spreadsheet formula or scheduled script will produce the same output every time; an AI agent won't. | The right pattern may change as you learn how AI performs on a task. Start with more oversight and relax as you build confidence. |
| **Level 4: Institutional Context** | **What Would Your Auditor Say?** | A practical gut-check. If an auditor would be uncomfortable with your method, that's a strong signal the task needs more auditability. | Imagine your institution's auditor reviewing this work. Would they ask "how was this done?" and expect a clear, documented answer? If yes, use auditable methods. If they'd only check the final result for accuracy, you have more flexibility with tools. | Auditor expectations vary by institution and by the specific audit. Use this as a directional guide, not a definitive test. |
| **Level 4: Institutional Context** | **Know Your Data Agreement** | Every AI tool has a data processing agreement, a contract that governs what happens to the information you provide. If you don't know what that agreement says, you don't know where your data is going, who can see it, or whether it's being used to train future models. | Before using any AI tool for work, answer: "Does my organization have a data processing agreement with this vendor?" and "Does this agreement cover the type of data I'm about to share?" Many free-tier tools state that inputs may be used for training; enterprise agreements typically offer stronger protections, but specifics vary. Check whether yours covers data retention, training exclusion, geographic restrictions, and who at the vendor can see your data. | Work with your IT, legal, or compliance team to understand what's covered. Don't assume that "our institution uses this tool" means "I can put anything into it." Approved tools often have approved use cases, and your use case may not be one of them. |
| **Level 4: Institutional Context** | **Does Your Office Have AI Policies?** | Institutional policies may already answer the question for you. If your office has guidelines on AI use, start there before making case-by-case judgments. | Check: Does your institution or department have a policy on using AI for this type of work? Many universities now have AI use policies that say which tasks can involve AI and which tools are approved. Following existing policy is both easier and safer than making individual calls. | As of October 2026, NIH, NSF, and NEH have written AI rules, so even where your office has no policy, the sponsor's notice may already bind you. Check it, then use judgment, which is what the rest of these principles help with. |
| **Level 4: Institutional Context** | **Consider Who Sees the Final Product** | The audience for the output determines how auditable the process needs to be. Internal working documents have different standards than sponsor deliverables. | Ask: "Who will see or use this output?" Internal brainstorming: Low Auditability is usually fine. Colleague review: still Low, but keep your sources. Sponsor submission: High, and check the sponsor's AI rules. External publication: High, plus any disclosure requirements. Match your method to your audience's expectations. | Audiences overlap. A draft that starts internal may end up in a sponsor submission. Consider the most demanding potential audience, not just the immediate one. |
| **Level 5: Strategic Positioning** | **Start Auditable and Loosen Up** | When you're unsure, begin with full auditability and relax as you learn. It's easier to loosen oversight than to explain why you skipped it. | For an unfamiliar task, treat it as High Auditability and use the Human leads, AI supports pattern. As you learn the accountability requirements, some tasks will turn out to be Low Auditability and AI can take on more of the work. Starting cautious protects you; starting loose creates risk. | Being overly cautious wastes time. The goal is appropriate auditability, not maximum restriction. Use this when genuinely unsure, not as a default for everything. |
| **Level 5: Strategic Positioning** | **Reassess as Policies Change** | Today's verdict won't be permanent. As tools improve, policies evolve, and your team gains experience, a task's verdict or usage pattern may change in either direction. | Schedule periodic reviews: "Is this still High Auditability, or have our tools and policies matured enough that AI can take a bigger role?" New sponsor notices may also tighten requirements (as of October 2026, NIH, NSF, and NEH all have AI rules). Stay current and adjust. | Reclassification needs deliberate review, not quiet drift. Don't let a task's verdict or usage pattern change without explicit discussion with the right people. |

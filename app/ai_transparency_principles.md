### How AI Models Work - A Reference Guide for Non-Experts

| Topic | What to Know | Why It Matters | Analogy | Key Resources |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **From Text to Tokens** | AI models don't read words - they break text into "tokens," which are roughly word fragments. "Understanding" becomes two tokens: "under" and "standing." The model works with these fragments as numbers, not as language. Different languages tokenize differently; English is efficient while other languages may use 2-4x more tokens for the same meaning. | Token count determines cost, speed, and how much information fits in a single request. When a tool charges "per token," this is what they mean. When people talk about "context window" limits, they mean token limits. | Imagine translating a book into Morse code before sending it by telegraph. The original meaning is there, but the telegraph doesn't understand English - it just transmits dots and dashes. Tokens are the AI's Morse code. | Try the OpenAI Tokenizer (platform.openai.com/tokenizer) to see how text gets split into tokens. |
| **Next-Token Prediction** | At its core, a language model does one thing: given everything it's seen so far, it predicts the most likely next token. It does this one token at a time, thousands of times per response. It doesn't plan ahead, outline its argument, or check its facts. It generates the next word that "fits." | This is why AI can sound perfectly fluent while being completely wrong. Fluency and accuracy are unrelated - the model optimizes for "what word comes next" not "what's true." This is also why longer, more specific prompts help: they constrain the prediction space. | Think of autocomplete on your phone, but trained on billions of pages of text instead of just your message history. It's incredibly good at predicting what plausible text looks like. But "plausible" and "correct" are different things. | |
| **Training vs. Inference** | Training is the expensive part - exposing the model to enormous amounts of text so it learns patterns. It happens once per model version and costs millions of dollars. Inference is when you actually use the trained model - sending it a prompt and getting a response. Each query is cheap, but there are billions a day. | Training data determines what the model "knows" and what biases it carries, and it has a cutoff date. Vendors train; everything your institution does is inference. Inference is now the majority of AI compute worldwide, so it is where the real environmental and cost questions live. | Training is like educating a medical student over years: expensive, time-consuming, done once. Inference is like that doctor seeing patients: each appointment uses the training but costs far less than the education did. | |
| **The Knowledge Cutoff** | The base model is trained on text up to a specific date and knows nothing after it. As of October 2026, ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude all search the web by default to fill that gap, so "it doesn't browse the internet" is no longer true. But search doesn't remove the problem: the model decides what to look up, skims it, and blends it with older training patterns. It may retrieve a stale page, or nothing at all, and still answer confidently. | Two failure modes now: a stale base model, and sloppy retrieval. Check what it actually retrieved - the cited pages and their dates - not just the summary. For any sponsor policy or regulation, read the current notice yourself. Veridex teaches that checking. | It's like a colleague back from a year's sabbatical who now carries a phone. They can look things up, but they'll skim the first result, and you won't know which one unless you ask. | |
| **Attention and Context Windows** | The "context window" is the total amount of text the model can consider at once - your prompt, any documents you include, and the conversation history. Everything in this window competes for the model's attention. Models pay more consistent attention to text at the beginning and end of the window; information in the middle can get less focus. | This directly affects how you work with AI. Long documents may not get fully processed. Important details buried in the middle of a large prompt may be missed. The context window also has a hard limit - exceed it and information gets silently dropped. | Think of the context window as a meeting room with a limited number of seats. Everyone you bring into the room gets to speak, but the more people present, the harder it is for any one voice to be heard. The people who spoke first and most recently tend to be remembered best. | |
| **Hallucination** | "Hallucination" (NIST calls it "confabulation") is when a model generates information that is plausible-sounding but fabricated - fake citations, invented statistics, nonexistent people or policies. Vendors have reduced it but cannot remove it, because it is a property of prediction: the model generates text that fits the pattern, whether or not it corresponds to reality. Web search helps with recency, not with this. | Never trust AI output on factual claims without verification. This is especially critical in research administration where policy details, regulatory requirements, and funding terms must be exact. Models will cite papers that don't exist, quote regulations with wrong section numbers, and invent institutional policies. Vitrea explains why this happens; Veridex teaches how to check. | Imagine asking someone to continue a story they've never read. They'll write something that sounds like it belongs, using the right style and vocabulary. But they're creating, not remembering. That's what a model does with facts - it generates what sounds right, not what it looked up. | NIST AI 600-1 (2024) "Generative AI Profile" (doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1). |
| **"Reasoning" Models** | Some newer models include a "thinking" or "reasoning" step where the model generates an internal chain of thought before responding. This improves performance on logic problems, math, and multi-step tasks. But this isn't reasoning the way humans reason - it's still pattern matching, just with an intermediate step that helps organize the output. | Reasoning models are better for complex, multi-step tasks but cost more and take longer. Do not treat the visible "thinking" as an audit trail. Anthropic (2025) gave models hints and found the trace mentioned the hint that actually changed the answer only 25 to 39 percent of the time. Many products also show a summary of the trace, not the trace itself. It is a readability aid, not evidence of how the answer was produced. | It's the difference between answering a math question off the top of your head versus writing out your work on scratch paper first. The scratch paper helps organize the approach, but a student can write one method on the paper and quietly use another to get the answer. | Anthropic (2025) "Reasoning models don't always say what they think" (anthropic.com/research/reasoning-models-dont-say-think). |
| **Open-Weight vs. Hosted Models** | A "hosted" model (Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT) runs only on the vendor's servers; you send data in and get responses back. An "open-weight" model (Llama, Mistral, Google's Gemma, OpenAI's gpt-oss) publishes the trained weights, so anyone can download and run it. Under the Open Source Initiative's definition, weights alone are not "open source" (training data and code usually stay private), so say "open-weight." As of October 2026, cloud vendors routinely host open-weight models too. | Open weights do not by themselves keep your data in-house. If a cloud vendor hosts the model, your prompts still go to that vendor; where they go depends on the hosting contract, not on whether the weights are public. Running a model on your own hardware does keep data local, but needs IT resources and expertise. Let your institution's data classification guide the choice. | A published recipe (open weights) means anyone could cook the dish. But if you order it from a restaurant, you are still trusting that kitchen with your order. What matters for your data is who is running the kitchen, not whether the recipe is public. | Open Source Initiative, "Open Source AI Definition" (opensource.org/ai/open-source-ai-definition). |
| **Where Your Data Goes** | Your prompt travels to remote servers; what happens next depends on the plan, not the brand. As of October 2026: Anthropic trains on Free, Pro and Max chats unless the user opts out, keeps data up to five years for those who allow it, and excludes Commercial, API and Education plans. OpenAI's consumer tiers train unless the user opts out; Business, Enterprise, Edu and API do not. Google's Gemini Apps chats may be read by human reviewers, and reviewed chats are kept up to three years even after you delete them; the Gemini API's unpaid tier uses inputs for improvement, the paid tier does not. A paid personal plan is not an enterprise plan. Policies change often - check the current page. | Ask three questions before pasting anything: Is this an institutional (enterprise or education) account, or my personal login? Does the plan prohibit training and human review? What data classification has IT security cleared for this tool? A personal Pro subscription paid with a p-card is still a consumer account. Your IT security office knows which tools have been vetted. | When you mail a letter, you trust the postal service not to read it - but the contract matters. Enterprise plans are like certified mail: tracked, with contractual guarantees. Consumer plans are like a postcard: it will probably arrive fine, but you have agreed it may be read along the way. | Anthropic consumer terms (anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms); OpenAI enterprise privacy (openai.com/enterprise-privacy); Gemini Apps privacy (support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961); Gemini API terms (ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms). Your institution's IT security office has the current approved-tool list. |
| **Sponsor and Regulatory Rules** | As of October 2026, the major sponsors have written rules. NIH will not treat applications substantially developed by AI as original work (NOT-OD-25-132, effective September 2025) and expects applicants to describe their AI use. NIH forbids reviewers from using AI to analyze applications or uploading any application content to an AI tool (NOT-OD-23-149, 2023); it is a confidentiality breach. NSF (notice first issued December 2023) tells reviewers not to upload proposal content to non-approved tools and treats doing so as potentially placing it in the public domain. Check the current notice for your sponsor. | Some data is regulated regardless of sponsor. Student records under FERPA can only go to a vendor acting as a "school official" under contractual control, which consumer tools lack. Export-controlled technical data placed in a cloud tool can be a deemed export. Protected health information under HIPAA needs a business associate agreement. In every case the anchor is your institution's data classification: know the category of what you hold before you choose a tool, and ask the sponsored programs office when in doubt. | Pasting a colleague's proposal into a chatbot is like reading it aloud in a hotel lobby. You no longer control who heard it, and the sponsor's rules assume you would. | NIH NOT-OD-23-149 (grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-23-149.html); NIH NOT-OD-25-132 (grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-25-132.html); NSF, AI in merit review (nsf.gov/policies/ai/merit-review). |
| **Environmental Impact: Training** | Training a frontier model consumes thousands of megawatt-hours of electricity plus water for cooling. Vendors do it, not you: once per model version, at a cost of millions, producing a model that millions then use. | Separate training from using for honest accounting. Training is a one-time cost spread across all users, but it is no longer the headline: inference - everyone's daily queries - is now the majority of AI compute, and it is the part that grows as adoption grows. Your institution's footprint is inference. | Training is like building a highway: enormously expensive, done once, used by millions. Nobody blames drivers for the construction cost - but once it exists, the traffic is what matters year after year. | IEA (2025) "Energy and AI" (iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai). MIT News (2025) "Explained: Generative AI's environmental impact" (news.mit.edu/2025/explained-generative-ai-environmental-impact-0117). |
| **Environmental Impact: Using AI** | As of 2025, Google reports a median Gemini text prompt uses about 0.24 watt-hours and OpenAI reports about 0.34 Wh per ChatGPT query - roughly a 10-watt LED bulb running for one to two minutes. Images, video and long "reasoning" responses cost much more. On top of the computing, the building adds overhead: an average data center uses about 1.5 units of energy for every 1 unit of computing (Uptime Institute 2025), while the big cloud operators report about 1.1 (Google). | Individually, a text query is small. Institutionally - thousands of staff making dozens of queries a day, increasingly with images and reasoning models - it adds up, and facility overhead depends on who hosts the model. Whether running AI on your own servers would be greener depends on your local grid and how busy those servers are - a judgment call for your IT and sustainability offices, not a settled fact. | Think of it like a light left on. One bulb for two minutes is nothing. A campus of bulbs, some of them floodlights, on all day is an electricity bill - and it costs more in a drafty building than a well-insulated one. | MIT Technology Review (2025) on Google's Gemini energy figures (technologyreview.com/2025/08/21/1122288/google-gemini-ai-energy/). Uptime Institute (2025) Global Data Center Survey (intelligence.uptimeinstitute.com/resource/uptime-institute-global-data-center-survey-2025). Google data center efficiency (datacenters.google/efficiency). |
| **Bias in Training Data** | Models learn patterns from their training data, including biases present in that data. If the training corpus over-represents certain perspectives, demographics, or writing styles, the model's outputs will reflect those skews. This isn't a simple problem to fix - bias can be subtle, contextual, and domain-specific. | For research contexts, this means AI output may reflect dominant cultural assumptions, underrepresent minority perspectives, or reproduce historical biases in language and framing. Anyone using AI for policy drafting, communications, or analysis should be aware that the tool's "neutral" output isn't actually neutral. | A model trained mostly on English-language academic text will write like an English-speaking academic - even when that voice isn't appropriate for the audience or context. It's not choosing that voice deliberately; it's the only voice it knows well. | NIST AI 600-1 (2024) "Generative AI Profile" (doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1). NIST AI Risk Management Framework (nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework). |
